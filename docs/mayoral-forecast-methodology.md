# How the mayoral forecast is made

The forecast on the homepage comes from the v3 compact model, fitted every time the polls are updated and published as forecast feed schema 5. It answers three questions from the same set of simulated elections: how far apart the two leading candidates are likely to finish, what each candidate's share of the vote could look like, and where the forecast's uncertainty comes from. This page explains the model in plain language. The formal record is ADR 0054, amended by ADR 0055 (election-day error), ADR 0056 (uncertainty breakdown), ADR 0057 (poll reading selection) and ADR 0061 (Chris Alexander's suspended campaign), and the research notes they cite.

## What goes in

- **This year's polls.** Each extracted citywide sample taken before October 6 enters once through a general vote-intention reading reporting Olivia Chow, Brad Bradford and Chris Alexander. Among eligible readings, the model prefers decided-and-leaning, then decided-only, then all respondents, then other source-defined cuts. Ties go to more modelled named candidates, then reading ID. A reading can qualify even if fieldwork preceded certification or the poll offered additional candidates. Readings of an earlier field missing one of the three are excluded from the main fit; a separate sensitivity fit admits readings with any two of them. Ward polls, blocked samples and context-only questions are excluded.
- **Polls since Alexander's exit.** Chris Alexander ended his campaign on October 6 and is still on the ballot. A poll whose fieldwork overlaps or follows that date enters as a comparison between Chow and Bradford, whether or not it still names him; any share it reports for him is set aside. When such a poll asks both the full field and a question offering only Chow and Bradford, the full-field question counts, whatever its denominator; the two-candidate question counts only when the poll asked nothing else. The date comes from the pinned Results release.
- **One reading's actual respondents.** The model selects from the released sample, reading and response tables, independently of the reading displayed in the public archive. Undecided and other responses are set aside and the named candidates' shares renormalised (Chow and Bradford alone after October 6). The reading's weighted base, otherwise reported base, otherwise unweighted base, times the summed named share sets its effective sample size. The recruited sample is a fallback only when no reading base is reported. Published source and archive values retain their original rounding.
- **Seven past campaigns.** The 2003, 2006, 2010, 2014, 2018, 2022 and 2023 Toronto mayoral races, with 98 polls and their results, read from the same Polling release as this year's polls. They are not used to predict 2026 directly. They teach the model four things: how much support typically moves from week to week, how far polling firms typically sit from one another, how far final polls have typically been from the result, and how much of the vote typically goes to minor candidates.
- **Ten past suspended campaigns.** Canadian mayoral candidates who ended a campaign after the ballot deadline but stayed on the ballot: Toronto 2010 (two), Calgary 2010 (three), London 2014, Montreal 2017, Sudbury 2022 and the Toronto 2023 by-election (two). The seven whose last poll gave them a measurable share kept between about 3% and 25% of it on election day. This record, not this year's polls, sets how much of his support Alexander keeps: pollsters stop naming a candidate after an exit, so polls cannot tell.
- **Nothing else.** No editorial adjustments, no fundamentals, no judgement about individual candidates, and no assumption about where Alexander's supporters go.

## What the model does

1. **Where the race stands.** Each candidate's support is tracked as a path through the campaign. Each poll is a noisy reading of that path, with allowance for the firm that ran it and for how many respondents are behind the chosen reading. Polls disagree, so the current estimate carries a range whose width depends on the evidence.
2. **What can still change.** Between the latest poll and election day the path keeps moving, at the pace the past campaigns say is typical for Toronto, with this campaign allowed to be calmer or livelier than average.
3. **Alexander's exit.** From October 6, Alexander keeps a fraction of his support drawn from the ten past cases, and the rest divides between Chow and Bradford. That split starts in proportion to their own support and is learned from the polls taken since October 6, each read against its firm's usual lean; while such polls are few, it stays wide. Nothing else is added at the exit: there is no hand-set jump. This applies only to this year's race; the seven past campaigns are fitted as before. In the simulations his election-day share is currently about half a percent.
4. **How polls miss.** On election day the result differs from the final polling picture. The model treats the result as one more, especially noisy, reading of the electorate, with the noise level learned from the seven past races, where the gap between the leader and the runner-up known at the time was off by about 16 points on average. Larger candidates move by more points than smaller ones, and a fringe candidate's proportional swings do not set the front-runners' uncertainty. This is the largest source of uncertainty in the forecast and the reason a clear polling lead does not translate into a near-certain win.
5. **The rest of the ballot.** All certified candidates outside the named three are treated as one pool whose combined share is learned from past races. Candidates individually reported by a poll can be listed in that pool without becoming separately modelled winners. The pool's candidate count comes from the pinned Results release. Alexander's freed support goes only to Chow and Bradford, so the pool's share is unchanged by his exit.

Steps 1 to 5 are combined into 16,000 simulated elections. Every number on the page is a summary of those simulations, so the margin chart, the vote ranges and the uncertainty rows always agree with one another.

## What the numbers mean

- **The compared candidates** are the two with the highest full-race win probabilities in the joint simulations. The feed supplies their identities; the presentation follows them.
- **The margin bars** sort the simulations into three outcomes: the leader ahead by two points or more, the two within two points of each other, and the challenger ahead by two or more. Each bar carries its share of the simulations. Finishing ahead of one rival is not the same as winning the race.
- **The vote ranges**, the first tab beneath the margin chart, are the middle 80% of simulated outcomes for each share of all votes cast: Chow, Bradford and Other candidates. Other candidates is the pool plus Alexander, added together within each simulation, so its range is not the sum of two ranges. Alexander stays a named candidate in the model and in the full-race win probabilities. Ranges can overlap; they are not chances of winning.
- **The lede's percentage** is how often the leader finishes ahead of the compared challenger across the simulations; chances are rounded to whole percentages, with "<1%" and ">99%" at the extremes. Full-race win probabilities instead count how often each named candidate beats every other named candidate.
- **Where the uncertainty comes from**, the second tab beneath the margin chart, shows the gap between the two leaders under each source of doubt on its own, applied to the middle estimate from the polls: the polls' own noise, campaign movement from the latest fieldwork to election day, and how far results have landed from final polls. A fourth row, set apart, is all three together, which is the published forecast. The number printed at the right is each source's variance as a fraction of the sum of the three component variances; the three add up to 100%. The ranges themselves do not add.
- **The forecast history** recomputes the race after each publication date using only the selected samples published by that date. A late release of older fieldwork enters on its publication date. These points use the current model and are not an archive of previously deployed forecasts. Fieldwork end places each current poll on the model's timeline. Points dated before October 6 have no exit to apply, so they are unchanged; later points apply it. The build's analysis cutoff is metadata; refreshing without new evidence does not shorten the movement between the latest poll and election day.

## What the model does not know

It cannot tell which way this year's polls are wrong, only how wrong polls have tended to be. It treats a late surge by a candidate outside the top two the same way past surges have played out, and there have been two in seven races. It has seven past elections to learn from, so its uncertainty about its own uncertainty is real. It knows where Alexander's supporters went only from the polls taken since October 6, and the share he keeps rests on seven past cases that no poll can check. When the model's numerical checks fail, the previous forecast stays up rather than a broken one being published.

## Checks

Before this model replaced the previous one it was tested by hiding each past election's result in turn and predicting it from the polls available 39 days out (the position of the 2026 race when the model was adopted) and 14 days out. Its 80% ranges for the leader's margin contained the actual margin in ten of eleven cases. The election-day noise model was then chosen by a second test whose pass rule was written down before the candidates were fitted; the version adopted predicted the past elections better than the one it replaced on every measure in that rule, and moved the poll leader's chance from about 63% to about 70% at adoption. The full records and alternatives tried and rejected are in the research notes referenced by ADR 0054 and ADR 0055.

The handling of Alexander's exit was chosen the same way. Each version was tested by hiding each past election in turn, 20 and 14 days out and across five random seeds, under a pass rule written down before it ran: its forecast of the leader's margin could be at most a tenth of a point worse on average than the current model's, its 80% range had to contain the actual margin in as many elections, and it could have no more sampling failures after one retry. The version adopted passed (0.06 and 0.01 points worse). On 2010, the one past race in which a candidate ended a campaign before polling stopped, it was the best of the versions tested a week out. Four alternatives were tried and not adopted, among them a fixed proportional split and counting Alexander with the other candidates in every poll; ADR 0061 records them.

Each release's main fit and recomputed history points use four chains of 1,000 warmup and 4,000 retained draws. Publication requires zero divergent transitions, R-hat below 1.01 and ESS at least 400 on non-constant coordinates; a divergent fit gets one retry at tighter acceptance. The isotropic-discrepancy and widened-field sensitivity fits accompany the release as audit metadata and do not determine probability-band availability. Earlier history points can be cached when their inputs, model code, numerical libraries and fit settings are unchanged, so total build time depends on the snapshots needing refits. The complete operational sequence is the [poll ingestion and release runbook](../../toronto-election-poll-tracker-data/docs/runbooks/add-2026-mayoral-poll.md).
